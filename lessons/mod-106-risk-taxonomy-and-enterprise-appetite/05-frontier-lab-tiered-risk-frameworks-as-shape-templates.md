# Frontier-lab tiered-risk frameworks — as shape templates, not templates to copy

## Why this chapter exists

The most rigorous publicly-documented tiered-risk frameworks in the AI industry today were authored by frontier labs, not by enterprises. Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP), OpenAI's Preparedness Framework, Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework (FSF), and the joint work published under the Frontier Model Forum's umbrella collectively define most of the current shared vocabulary for capability thresholds, evaluation gates, deployment mitigations, security levels, and pre-registered rollback commitments. Any enterprise architect authoring an appetite architecture (chapter 03) and an aggregation model (chapter 04) is going to be asked "why not just adopt what Anthropic and OpenAI do?" by the head of AI governance, by the audit committee, and — depending on how far up the enterprise sits — by the board.

The answer is not "we should not" and it is not "we should." The answer is: **the frontier-lab frameworks are the best current shape templates for how a tiered-risk framework can be structured with real teeth, but they are engineered against a scope the enterprise does not share.** Frontier labs are producing the models the enterprise deploys; the enterprise is deploying models the frontier labs produce plus first-party fine-tunes and applications. The failure modes differ, the thresholds differ, the mitigation portfolio differs. The architect reads the frontier-lab frameworks for their *architectural moves* — pre-registration, capability tripwires, deployment / security level pairing, rollback commitments, external accountability — and adapts each move to enterprise scope rather than lifting them wholesale.

This chapter walks the four frameworks at architectural altitude, extracts the six moves the enterprise adopts, names the four moves the enterprise adapts substantially, and identifies one move the enterprise usually does not adopt at all. It is not an exhaustive read of any framework — for that, read the frameworks themselves; the resources file points to the current published versions. It is a shape lesson.

## What the four frameworks share

Before differences, the shared shape is worth naming. All four frameworks (Anthropic RSP, OpenAI Preparedness, DeepMind FSF, Frontier Model Forum publications) share the following architectural moves:

- **Pre-registered capability thresholds.** Before a model reaches a certain capability level, the lab commits publicly (or at least to a defined governance body) to specific evaluations and specific mitigations that will apply at that level. The commitment is *before* the capability is reached; the lab does not decide after the fact what "sufficient mitigation" looked like.
- **Evaluation gates.** Before crossing a threshold, a defined set of evaluations must be run and passed. The evaluations are named in advance; the pass criteria are named in advance.
- **Deployment and security level pairing.** Mitigations are decomposed into deployment mitigations (how the model is exposed to users) and security mitigations (how the model, weights, code, and eval infrastructure are protected). Each threshold names required levels of both.
- **Rollback and stop-shipping commitments.** If evaluations fail, if a mitigation is not achieved in time, if a novel dangerous capability emerges, the lab commits in advance to specific rollback actions: paused training, paused deployment, restricted access, model withdrawal. The commitments have real weight because they are ratified before the pressure arrives.
- **External accountability.** Frameworks are published; updates are published; failures against the framework's own thresholds are (nominally) disclosed. The external audience is other labs, governments, standards bodies, and — increasingly — customers and civil-society researchers.
- **Explicit versioning.** Frameworks version. Each version supersedes the previous; the changes are enumerated; the migration between versions is documented.

All six moves are architectural moves the enterprise can borrow. The next section walks them one at a time.

## The four frameworks at architectural altitude

**Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP).** The RSP defines a set of AI Safety Levels (ASL) analogous in shape to biosafety levels (BSL). Each ASL specifies (a) the *capability threshold* that triggers the level — evaluated via named model evaluations for tracked dangerous capabilities such as biosecurity, cybersecurity, autonomy, and others; (b) the *Deployment Standard* required at that level, defining the constraints on how the model is deployed; and (c) the *Security Standard* required at that level, defining the constraints on how the weights, code, and eval infrastructure are protected. The RSP has an explicit *pre-registered* structure: evaluations are named in advance; the transition from one ASL to the next requires evaluations to pass and both standards to be met. If both cannot be met, deployment or training pauses. The RSP has been revised multiple times; each revision is versioned.

**OpenAI's Preparedness Framework.** The Preparedness Framework specifies *tracked risk categories* (e.g., cybersecurity, CBRN, persuasion, model autonomy — the specific list has been updated across framework versions) and *risk levels* per category (low / medium / high / critical, though the framework's own labels have evolved). For each risk category and level, the framework defines the evaluations that determine which level a model has reached, the mitigations required to deploy at that level, and the actions if the level would be crossed without mitigation being ready. The framework carries an explicit *Safety Advisory Group* structure — an internal body that reviews evaluations and makes level assignments — and specifies pre-deployment gates before deploying at higher risk levels.

**Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework (FSF).** The FSF names *Critical Capability Levels* (CCLs) — thresholds at which a model's capabilities in specific domains (autonomy, biosecurity, cybersecurity, ML R&D, and others across framework revisions) would create a meaningful risk of severe harm absent mitigations. For each CCL, the FSF specifies the *early warning evaluations* that predict approaching the level, the *response* required if the level is reached (deployment mitigations, security mitigations, potentially pausing further capability increases), and the *governance* — internal review, sometimes external review — that must occur before deployment.

**Frontier Model Forum publications.** The Frontier Model Forum — a consortium of Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, and OpenAI, with observers — has published on shared vocabulary for safety evaluations, transparency reports, and best practices. The Forum's contribution to the shape lesson is the *interoperability move*: labs converging on similar naming and thresholds so cross-lab and cross-jurisdiction comparison is possible. For the enterprise architect, the Forum's material is best read as a coordination artefact rather than a framework to adopt directly.

## The six moves the enterprise adopts

The enterprise's tiered-risk architecture legitimately adopts six of the frontier-lab moves. These map onto the four artefacts of the appetite architecture (chapter 03) and the aggregation model (chapter 04).

**Move 1 — Pre-registration of thresholds.** The enterprise's tolerance table (chapter 03 artefact 2) and stop-shipping thresholds (artefact 4) are authored and ratified *before* the systems that would breach them are deployed. Retroactive tolerance adjustments — "the score exceeded appetite; we revised appetite to accommodate the score" — are the enterprise-scale version of what the RSP and Preparedness Framework guard against. Pre-registration is the enterprise's most direct import.

**Move 2 — Named-in-advance evaluations for each threshold.** For each capability tier (chapter 02) and each category-tier tolerance cell (chapter 03), the enterprise names in advance the evaluations that will produce the residual score. The evaluation set per tier is enumerated in the aggregation model specification; the risk engineer (level 25) implements against the named set; the evaluation engineer (peer level 30) calibrates the methodology (chapter 06). Ad hoc "we'll figure out what evals to run at deployment time" is the enterprise-scale equivalent of what the frontier-lab frameworks guard against.

**Move 3 — Deployment and security level pairing.** The enterprise's control library (mod-102) already carries controls in the `AIC-HOV-*` (oversight), `AIC-SEC-*` (security), `AIC-ROB-*` (robustness), and `AIC-TRP-*` (transparency) families. The tolerance table's cells reference the control level required per (category, tier) — an enterprise's tier-3 cell for `confidential-information-exfiltration` might require both a query-budget deployment control and a model-weight encryption security control. Pairing deployment-side and security-side controls per cell rather than at a single "controls needed" level is the enterprise adaptation of the RSP's Deployment / Security Standard split.

**Move 4 — Rollback and stop-shipping commitments.** The stop-shipping thresholds artefact (chapter 03 artefact 4) is the enterprise-scale equivalent of the frontier-lab pre-registered pause commitments. The two differ in scale (a frontier lab pausing training of a foundation model is a different economic decision than an enterprise pausing deployment of a fine-tuned assistant) but the *architecture* is the same: pre-registered conditions, ratified in advance, invocable without a manager-level override. Chapter 03 already argued for this shape.

**Move 5 — Explicit versioning.** The taxonomy versioning (chapter 07), tolerance table versioning, aggregation model versioning are all imports from the frontier-lab practice of shipping frameworks with real version numbers, migration paths, and change enumerations. The alternative — the enterprise's tolerance table drifting via untracked edits — is what turns a governance artefact into a document nobody trusts within a year.

**Move 6 — Internal review body with defined authority.** The Preparedness Framework's Safety Advisory Group and the RSP's ratification structure both name a specific internal body that reviews evaluations and authorises level assignments (or the enterprise-equivalent decision). The enterprise's AI risk committee, model risk committee, or AI governance council (mod-112 will build the org shape) plays this role. The architect's contribution here is to name the body's authority clearly — what decisions it makes, what evidence it reads, what happens when it disagrees with the system owner — rather than leaving the decision authority ambiguous.

## The four moves the enterprise adapts substantially

Not everything from the frontier-lab frameworks survives naïve transplantation. Four moves are adapted rather than adopted.

**Adaptation 1 — the capability threshold definition.** Frontier-lab thresholds are defined in terms of the *model's underlying capability* — a threshold is "the model can autonomously exfiltrate a code repository given zero-shot access" or "the model can uplift a novice on CBRN synthesis by a defined margin over baseline access to the internet." The enterprise, by contrast, mostly is not training foundation models. Its capability tiers (chapter 02) are properties of the *system as deployed* — action authority, data blast radius, autonomy horizon — not the underlying model. The enterprise adapts the capability-threshold move by defining thresholds in *system-deployment-attribute* terms. The frontier-lab and enterprise definitions can be layered — a system with a tier-4 deployment attribute using a base model with an RSP tier-N capability produces a compound assessment — but the enterprise's primary threshold surface is system-deployment, not model-capability.

**Adaptation 2 — the eval set.** Frontier labs run capability evaluations of their own foundation models across dangerous-capability categories (biosecurity uplift, cyber uplift, autonomy, persuasion, and so on). The enterprise usually does not; the enterprise runs *system-level* evaluations of prompts, guardrails, retrieval quality, tool-invocation safety, fairness in decisions, robustness under adversarial input. The eval set the enterprise names for each tier-cell is a system-level eval set, not a foundation-model capability eval set. Where the enterprise consumes foundation-model capability evaluations from the model provider, that is a *third-party attestation* artefact into the enterprise's evidence architecture (mod-108) — one input among several — not the enterprise's own primary evaluation. Chapter 06 detailings the scoring contract this produces.

**Adaptation 3 — external accountability.** Frontier labs publish their frameworks and (nominally) their failures. The enterprise usually cannot — its risk register is confidential by default, its tolerance table is not typically a public artefact, its stop-shipping thresholds may be described at a high level in regulatory filings but not in operational detail. The external-accountability move at enterprise scope means *audit-committee accountability, regulator-facing accountability where regulation attaches, and customer-facing accountability at a summary level* rather than public publication. The mod-107 assurance architecture and mod-108 evidence architecture will carry this; the point here is that the enterprise version of external accountability is smaller and more segmented than the frontier-lab version.

**Adaptation 4 — the safety-adjacent research programme.** Frontier-lab frameworks assume the lab is *doing the research* to develop the evaluations, to characterise the dangerous capabilities, and to red-team the models. The enterprise is not. The enterprise consumes evaluation methodologies developed by the labs, by NIST US AISI, by UK AISI, by academic groups, and by the vendors that build AI risk-engineering platforms. The adaptation is that the enterprise's risk engineer (level 25) and evaluation engineer (peer level 30) are *implementers and calibrators*, not primary researchers. The taxonomy (chapter 02) and aggregation model (chapter 04) must be designed to consume evaluation results from external methodologies without pretending the enterprise developed them.

## The one move the enterprise usually does not adopt

**The moratorium on further capability development.** RSP and the Preparedness Framework contemplate a pause in *further model training* if capability thresholds are approached without mitigations being ready. The enterprise usually is not training foundation models and cannot pause the underlying model development — that decision sits with the model provider. The enterprise-equivalent move is to *pause deployment* or to *restrict use*, which the stop-shipping thresholds (chapter 03 artefact 4) already carry. The training-pause move is not a lever the enterprise has.

Where the enterprise *is* doing fine-tuning or continuous pre-training on foundation models (increasingly common), a scoped equivalent may apply — a commitment to pause fine-tuning on certain domains until an evaluation is available. That is a defensible enterprise adaptation for enterprises doing that kind of work. Most enterprises are not, and the move is not universally applicable.

## The failure mode of naïve transplantation

The single most common mistake made by enterprises trying to adopt frontier-lab frameworks is to import the *capability-threshold structure* — the ASL-1/2/3/4 layering or the low/medium/high/critical risk-level layering — and apply it to the enterprise's own system deployments *without* re-scoping the capabilities that define the thresholds. The result is an enterprise appetite architecture that reads plausibly but that has no defensible mapping from its thresholds to its actual system fleet: no enterprise-deployed system is characterisable as "ASL-3" in any well-defined sense because ASL-3 is a property of the underlying foundation model and its capabilities in specific dangerous-capability domains, not a property of a system deploying such a model in a bounded enterprise context.

The taxonomy the enterprise ships must be *sourced* from the enterprise's own capability-tier definitions (chapter 02, axis 2) that align to its actual footprint — modality access, action authority, data blast radius, autonomy horizon. It can *reference* the frontier-lab thresholds as inputs (a tier-4 enterprise system using an ASL-3 foundation model inherits some of the ASL-3 mitigation requirements from the model provider's contract). It cannot *substitute* frontier-lab thresholds for its own; the two axes are not the same axis and conflating them makes the enterprise's appetite architecture opaque to its own reviewers.

## What to bring to the audit committee

When the audit committee asks "why not just do what Anthropic and OpenAI do?", the architect's answer is a version of this chapter, distilled:

- We *do* adopt the pre-registration, named-eval, pairing of deployment and security controls, rollback commitment, versioning, and internal-review-body moves. Chapter 03 and chapter 04's artefacts carry those.
- We *adapt* the capability-threshold definition (ours is deployment-attribute-driven, not model-capability-driven), the eval set (ours is system-level, not foundation-model-capability-level), the external-accountability shape (ours is audit-committee-and-regulator, not public), and the research programme (we consume; we do not primary-research).
- We *do not* adopt the training-pause move because we do not train the underlying models. Where we do fine-tune, we carry a scoped equivalent for the categories where it applies.

That answer takes ninety seconds, closes the question, and sets up the audit-committee-facing artefact chapter 07 will produce with its versioning discipline. Exercise-04 walks the adaptation of a specific frontier-lab framework to a specific enterprise scenario, so the architect can practise the answer against real material rather than in the abstract.

## Summary

Frontier-lab tiered-risk frameworks — Anthropic RSP, OpenAI Preparedness, Google DeepMind FSF, Frontier Model Forum publications — are the most rigorous publicly-documented shape templates the enterprise architect can read. The enterprise adopts six moves from them (pre-registration, named-in-advance evals, deployment-and-security pairing, rollback commitments, explicit versioning, internal review body with defined authority), adapts four moves substantially (capability threshold definition, eval set, external accountability, safety-adjacent research programme), and typically does not adopt the training-pause move because the enterprise usually does not train the underlying model. The failure mode to design against is naïve transplantation — importing the ASL-1/2/3/4 or low/medium/high/critical structure and applying it to enterprise systems without re-scoping the capability definition. The frontier-lab frameworks are shape templates; the enterprise's taxonomy (chapter 02), appetite architecture (chapter 03), and aggregation model (chapter 04) are what actually ship. Chapter 06 walks the quantification contract that produces the residuals the whole system operates on; chapter 07 walks the versioning practice the frontier-lab frameworks pioneered and the enterprise inherits.
