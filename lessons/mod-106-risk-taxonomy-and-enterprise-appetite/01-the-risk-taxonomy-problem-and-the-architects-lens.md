# The risk taxonomy problem — and the architect's lens on it

## Why this chapter exists

An enterprise that ships AI systems accumulates *risk statements* faster than any other artefact. The Monday launch review says the customer-facing chat assistant carries a "hallucination risk." The quarterly model-validation report says the fraud classifier carries a "drift risk." The internal audit finding says the RAG assistant carries a "prompt-injection risk." The board deck says the enterprise carries a "reputational risk from AI misuse." All four are true; none of them is *comparable*. "Hallucination risk," "drift risk," "prompt-injection risk," and "reputational risk from AI misuse" are drawn from four different vocabularies (product-team, model-validation, red-team, board-communications), sit at four different altitudes, and have four different implicit scoring shapes. Roll them onto a portfolio view and the portfolio view is a hallucination.

The risk taxonomy is the closed-world vocabulary that fixes this. It names the categories of harm the enterprise recognises, the capability tiers of the systems that can produce them, and the dependency map of how one risk conditions another. Every risk register entry, every incident classification, every appetite-tolerance row, every monitoring alarm, and every board metric references the taxonomy. The taxonomy is what makes the portfolio view a real view — a count of tier-2 systems currently carrying an above-appetite residual on the "confidential-information-exfiltration" category is a defensible sentence; a count of "AI risks" is not.

The level-50 architect owns the *shape* of this taxonomy: what categories exist, what their definitions are, what their relationships are, how they compose with the reference corpora (NIST AI RMF Generative AI Profile categories, ISO/IEC 23894 risk sources, AIRO ontology categories, MIT AI Risk Repository meta-taxonomy, AIID / OECD.AI incident classes), and how the taxonomy versions. The architect does *not* score individual risks against the taxonomy — that is `ai-risk-engineer` at level 25. The architect does *not* set the appetite — that is the board and audit committee, informed by `head-of-ai-governance` at level 60. The architect does *not* run the calibration studies that keep scoring consistent across raters — that is `model-evaluation-engineer` at peer level 30. The architect designs the *shape* the other three roles fill in.

This chapter frames the taxonomy as an architectural artefact distinct from the control library and from the incident classification scheme, walks the six invariants a workable taxonomy holds, and names the two failure modes that most first-cut enterprise AI risk taxonomies fall into.

## What the taxonomy is *not*

**Not the control library.** Mod-102 built the enterprise AI control library — the catalog of practices the enterprise runs, each with an applicability filter and an evidence contract. Controls are the *countermeasures*; risk categories are the *harms the countermeasures address*. An `AIC-ROB-*` control for adversarial-robustness testing is not itself a risk category; it *mitigates* risks in the "confidential-information-exfiltration" category (via extraction attacks), the "unsafe-model-behaviour-under-attack" category (via evasion), and possibly the "availability" category (via denial). One control maps to many risks; one risk is mitigated by many controls. Collapsing the two is the single most common failure mode of enterprise AI risk programmes and is where "we have a control for that" gets casually substituted for "we have quantified the residual against appetite for that." Mod-102 chapter 01 warned about this from the control side; this module enforces the separation from the risk side.

**Not the incident classification scheme.** Mod-110 (post-market surveillance) will build the enterprise incident classification scheme — the closed-world vocabulary the SOC and the AI incident-response team use to log what actually happened. Incidents realise risks; risks precede incidents. The two schemes must *interoperate* — an incident of type "prompt-injection succeeded and produced unauthorised tool call" should reference the "confidential-information-exfiltration" and "unsafe-agentic-action" risk categories — but they are not the same artefact. The incident scheme is populated by *observation* (what happened); the taxonomy is populated by *foresight* (what could happen). AIID and OECD.AI incident classifications are inputs to the taxonomy design (they tell you what has actually happened in the ecosystem) but not the taxonomy itself.

**Not the regulatory obligation register.** Mod-104 built the reconciliation architecture for regulatory obligations. Obligations are what the *law* demands; risk categories are what the *enterprise decides are worth reasoning about*. There is a substantial overlap — the EU AI Act Article 9 risk-management system references risks to health, safety, and fundamental rights; NIST AI RMF names harms to people, organisations, and ecosystems; ISO/IEC 42005 impact assessments name harms to individuals, groups, and society. But the enterprise taxonomy also carries categories that no regulator names (e.g., "commercial-partner-reputation-contagion" if the enterprise sells B2B AI into regulated industries) and legitimately declines to enumerate categories that regulators do name if they are out of scope for the enterprise's business model (e.g., "law-enforcement-use-case-scope-creep" for an enterprise that never sells to law-enforcement customers). The taxonomy is the enterprise's, informed by the regulatory register.

**Not the harm ontology from academia.** The MIT AI Risk Repository (which meta-analyses 43 taxonomies and 777 risks as of the 2024 v1 publication and updates on a rolling basis) and the AIRO (AI Risk Ontology) developed under the VAIR project are extraordinary reference corpora — they will teach the architect what categories the field has considered. They are not, in themselves, the enterprise taxonomy. Adopting either verbatim produces a 700-plus-category taxonomy that no one can operate against. The architect *composes* from them; the architect does not *inherit* them.

## The six invariants of a workable taxonomy

Every design decision in the chapters that follow — the categorisation axes (chapter 02), the appetite translation (chapter 03), the aggregation shape (chapter 04), the frontier-lab shape adaptations (chapter 05), the quantification contract (chapter 06), the versioning process (chapter 07) — is a choice about how to preserve these invariants. Break any one of them and the taxonomy degenerates.

**Invariant 1 — closed-world enumeration.** The taxonomy names a finite set of categories and forbids free-text alternatives. When a risk that does not fit any category shows up, the response is *file a taxonomy amendment* (chapter 07), not *paste a paragraph into the risk register*. This is the same closed-world convention mod-102 imposed on the control library's applicability filter and mod-103 imposed on the policy vocabulary. It is what makes the count queries ("how many tier-2 systems carry an above-appetite residual in category X?") return truthful answers.

**Invariant 2 — MECE within each axis.** Within each classification axis the taxonomy uses (harm category, capability tier, deployment context, and so on — chapter 02 argues for three primary axes), the values must be *mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive*. If a single risk plausibly belongs in three harm categories at once, the categories are not mutually exclusive and the counts double-count. If a risk plausibly belongs in none, the categories are not exhaustive and the risk goes unscored. MECE-ness is testable: take a representative sample of AIID incidents, ask two annotators to categorise them independently, and measure agreement (chapter 06 walks the calibration methodology). Agreement below a stated threshold is a taxonomy defect.

**Invariant 3 — separation of *what* from *how much*.** The taxonomy names the categories. It does *not* attach a fixed severity to any category. "Confidential-information-exfiltration" is a category; the score for a specific instance of that category on a specific system is set by the impact × likelihood × exposure quantification (chapter 06) and is variable. A taxonomy that hard-codes severity ("category X is always high") collapses into a compliance checklist and stops being useful for prioritisation. This is why the taxonomy and the appetite architecture (chapter 03) are separate artefacts — the taxonomy is the vocabulary, the appetite is the tolerance.

**Invariant 4 — capability-tier orthogonality.** A separate axis of the taxonomy is the *capability tier* of the system: what modalities it accepts, what actions it can take, what data it has access to, what autonomy it exercises. Capability tier is orthogonal to harm category — the same "confidential-information-exfiltration" category attaches very differently to a tier-1 read-only classifier than to a tier-4 agent with tool-calling access to internal APIs. Chapter 02 argues the shape of the capability tier axis; chapter 05 shows how the frontier-lab tiered frameworks operate on this axis explicitly.

**Invariant 5 — dependency map, not just a flat list.** Risks condition risks. A "training-data-poisoning" risk makes a "confidential-information-exfiltration-via-membership-inference" risk more likely and more consequential. A "prompt-injection" risk on an agent with tool-calling access can *cause* an "unsafe-agentic-action" risk, which can *cause* a "confidential-information-exfiltration" risk, which can *cause* a "regulatory-notification-obligation" risk. The taxonomy must carry these dependency edges — the aggregation model (chapter 04) uses them to detect cascade paths and to prevent double-counting when a residual on the downstream risk is a re-expression of the upstream one. A flat list of independent categories is architecturally cheaper on day one and misleads at the portfolio level thereafter.

**Invariant 6 — versionable, migratable, deprecatable.** The taxonomy is not static. The MIT AI Risk Repository has surfaced new risk categories every year since publication as agentic architectures, GPAI systems, and new attack techniques have emerged. Regulations add new categories (EU AI Act's Article 50 transparency obligations create a "synthetic-media-attribution" category the taxonomy in 2022 did not need). Frontier labs publish new dangerous-capability categories the enterprise should track. The taxonomy has semver-shaped versions, a change-management process, and a documented migration path for historical register entries. Chapter 07 details this; the point here is that if you cannot version and migrate, you cannot maintain, and the taxonomy will silently rot.

## The two failure modes to design against

**Failure mode 1 — the free-text risk register.** The enterprise stands up a governance council, publishes a risk-register template with a `risk_description` free-text column, and lets each product team fill it in. Within a quarter there are 200 entries, no two of which are comparable. The chief risk officer asks "how many of our AI systems currently carry residual risk in the confidential-information-exfiltration category above our tier-2 tolerance?" and the answer is "the risk register does not know." The failure is *not* that the teams wrote things down badly. The failure is that the enterprise never authored a taxonomy the register could reference. The taxonomy is what makes the register queryable, comparable, and aggregable. This chapter, and this module, exist to prevent this failure mode.

**Failure mode 2 — the imported-verbatim MIT/AIRO/NIST taxonomy.** The alternative mistake: the enterprise imports the MIT AI Risk Repository's meta-taxonomy (or AIRO, or NIST GenAI Profile categories) verbatim, publishes 700 categories, and hands the register to product teams. Nobody can score anything against 700 categories. Categories overlap (violating invariant 2 — MECE); categories that do not apply to the enterprise's actual footprint clutter the register (violating invariant 1 — closed-world enumeration only for what the enterprise commits to); the calibration effort is impossible (violating invariant 6 — the taxonomy cannot be operated). Within a quarter product teams stop populating anything except a top-level "AI risk" category, and the enterprise is back to the free-text failure mode with a heavier catalog to maintain. The reference corpora are *inputs* to the taxonomy design; they are not the taxonomy. Chapter 02 walks the composition move that avoids this.

## The architect's lens

When the enterprise's chief risk officer, head of AI governance, or model risk committee asks the architect a taxonomy-shaped question — "should we add a new category for agentic-tool-misuse?", "does the training-data-poisoning category subsume the model-extraction category?", "how do we compare our post-market surveillance signal against our appetite when the categories differ?" — the architect's lens is always the same:

1. **Which invariant is at stake?** Category proliferation questions are usually invariant 1 (closed-world) or invariant 2 (MECE). Severity-hard-coding questions are invariant 3. Agentic-tool-misuse questions are usually invariant 4 (capability-tier orthogonality — the risk is not novel, but the capability tier that carries it is). Cascade questions are invariant 5. Maintenance questions are invariant 6.
2. **Which reference corpus already speaks to this?** Chapter 02 pins the composition move — the NIST GenAI Profile categories, ISO/IEC 23894 risk sources, AIRO categories, MIT Repository meta-taxonomy, and AIID / OECD.AI incident classes. Before proposing a new enterprise category, check whether the reference corpora already carry a category the enterprise can adopt-or-adapt.
3. **What are the second-order consequences on aggregation, appetite, quantification, and versioning?** A new category ripples into the appetite tolerance table (chapter 03), the aggregation model (chapter 04), the quantification scoring (chapter 06), and the migration plan (chapter 07). The architect thinks four artefacts ahead when proposing a change.
4. **Who ratifies?** Taxonomy changes are ratified by the head of AI governance (level 60), on the architect's recommendation, and communicated per the change-communications process the taxonomy release carries. The architect does not publish unilaterally.

## Summary

The enterprise AI risk taxonomy is the closed-world vocabulary the enterprise commits to reason about AI risk within — distinct from the control library, from the incident classification scheme, from the regulatory obligation register, and from the academic harm ontology. It holds six invariants: closed-world enumeration, MECE within each axis, separation of what-from-how-much, capability-tier orthogonality, dependency map (not flat list), and versionable-migratable-deprecatable. The two failure modes to design against are the free-text risk register (which never authored a taxonomy) and the imported-verbatim MIT/AIRO taxonomy (which authored one too big to operate). The level-50 architect owns the taxonomy's shape; the level-25 risk engineer scores against it; the level-60 head of AI governance ratifies; the level-30 evaluation engineer calibrates. The next six chapters walk the composition (chapter 02), the appetite translation (chapter 03), the aggregation model (chapter 04), the frontier-lab shape lessons (chapter 05), the quantification contract (chapter 06), and the versioning + migration plan (chapter 07) — and land in a taxonomy + appetite pair the rest of the track (mod-107 assurance, mod-108 evidence, mod-110 monitoring, mod-111 GRC platform) can bind to.
