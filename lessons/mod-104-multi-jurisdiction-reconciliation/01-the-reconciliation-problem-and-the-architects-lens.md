# The reconciliation problem — and the architect's lens on it

## Why this chapter exists

Every AI system the enterprise ships is, from a compliance point of view, subject to *more than one* regime. The same customer-facing chat assistant is regulated by the EU AI Act (in Frankfurt), by California's SB 942 and AB 2013 (in Los Angeles), by NYC Local Law 144 (if it screens candidates), by GDPR Article 22 wherever the user is European, by the UK's transparency guidance in London, and — for the fraction of the assistant's traffic that involves a UK public-sector customer — by ATRS. The provider is also subject to the G7 Hiroshima Process Code of Conduct because the group signed it, and by the Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI because the parent company operates in a signatory state. A naïve reading says the assistant needs 60-plus distinct compliance programmes. That reading fails on cost, on coherence, and on staff sanity within a quarter.

The reconciliation architecture is the answer to the naïve reading. It says: *the enterprise runs one control library (mod-102); every regulatory obligation, wherever it comes from, is mapped onto controls in that library; the applicability filter on each control names the jurisdictions and use cases that turn the obligation on; the evidence contract on each control specifies what auditors from any regime will accept; and the deprecation path on each obligation states what happens when the source regulation changes.* The naïve 60 programmes become one library with a filter, evaluated per system.

The level-50 architect does not draft the regulations, does not lobby them, and does not litigate their interpretation — that is legal's and government-affairs' domain. The architect *reads* them as architectural inputs, decomposes each into obligations the control library can carry, decides which are *net-new* controls versus *filter-only additions to existing controls*, and publishes the mapping so the ai-risk-engineer (level 25) can implement, the ai-governance-analyst (level 15) can operate, and internal audit can test. This chapter frames that lens; the seven chapters that follow apply it, jurisdiction by jurisdiction, and land in a common reconciliation architecture (chapter `07-designing-the-reconciliation-architecture.md`) and the presumption-of-conformity end-state (chapter `08-cen-cenelec-jtc-21-and-the-future-state.md`).

## The four things reconciliation is not

**Not "pick the strictest and comply with that everywhere."** The intuition is appealing and wrong. Strictness is not a total order — the EU AI Act's technical-documentation obligations are strict, but China's Interim Measures for Generative AI include a *pre-launch security assessment* whose shape has no EU AI Act analogue; Colorado's algorithmic-discrimination duty attaches to a "consequential decision" definition that no other regime uses; Korea's AI Basic Act carries penalty exposure for GPAI thresholds different from the EU's. "Strictest" collapses these incomparables. It also under-reports evidence — a regime that requires you to *tell the regulator* something (Article 73 serious-incident reports) is not satisfied by a stricter regime that requires you to *retain* it (Article 12 logs). The reconciliation architecture treats each obligation individually and lets the filter decide who sees which evidence.

**Not "one control per regulation."** A 1:1 mapping from regulation to control produces catalogue bloat, duplication, and drift. NIST AI RMF's MEASURE 2.7, EU AI Act Article 15, and California CPPA ADMT risk-assessment provisions all bear on adversarial-robustness testing; the library carries one control for adversarial-robustness testing and three crosswalk edges, not three controls. Chapter `07-designing-the-reconciliation-architecture.md` shows the discipline. The failure mode of one-per-regulation is what mod-102 chapter 02 warned about — it turns the library from a governance instrument into a compliance ledger, and it hides the fact that *the underlying practice is the same regardless of which regime asks about it*.

**Not "harmonise the regimes."** The architect has no lever to change EU or US or Chinese law. Attempts to "advocate for a global standard" belong in industry-consortium work, government-affairs briefings, and the CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 process (chapter `08-cen-cenelec-jtc-21-and-the-future-state.md`). They do not belong in the reconciliation architecture. The architecture accepts the regimes as they are, reconciles their obligations onto shared controls, and lets the applicability filter carry the difference.

**Not "let legal own it."** Legal owns interpretation, enforceability, penalty exposure, and privileged advice. Legal does *not* own the control library or the evidence contract — those are architectural artefacts owned by the level-50 seat. The two roles collaborate at fixed interfaces: legal delivers an interpretation of an obligation (what does "algorithmic discrimination" mean under Colorado SB24-205 for a fraud model?), the architect delivers a control-and-evidence design that discharges the obligation given that interpretation. When either role tries to do the other's job, the programme slows and the artefacts get worse.

## The four axes every obligation moves along

Every regulatory obligation the architect reads has four independent attributes. Treating them as one collapses the reconciliation architecture; treating them separately is what lets it scale.

**Axis 1 — Who the addressee is.** Obligations attach to a legal role: *provider* of an AI system (the party that develops and places it on the market under its own name), *deployer* (the party that uses it under its own authority), *importer*, *distributor*, *authorised representative*, *user*. The EU AI Act names all six explicitly. Colorado uses *developer* and *deployer*. NYC LL 144 attaches to the *employer* using the tool. GDPR uses *controller* and *processor*. The reconciliation architecture must know the enterprise's role for each system — one system, four regimes, potentially four different addressee mappings, potentially four different obligations. The applicability filter on a control carries the addressee dimension, not just the jurisdiction.

**Axis 2 — What triggers the obligation.** Some obligations trigger on *system classification* (high-risk under EU AI Act, "consequential decision" under Colorado, "employment decision" under NYC LL 144). Others trigger on *use case* (biometric identification, credit scoring, education admissions). Others on *scale* (GPAI training-compute thresholds, "large" data controllers). Others on *event* (serious incident, breach, model retraining). Others on *place of user* (GDPR extraterritorial reach). Others on *counterparty* (US federal agency procuring under OMB M-25-21). Every applicability filter dimension in mod-102 chapter 01 (`system_tier`, `system_kind`, `use_case`, `jurisdiction`, `data_class`, ...) exists because some regime somewhere triggers on it. The filter is the reconciliation architecture's primary tool for making obligations conditional.

**Axis 3 — What the obligation demands.** Obligations demand different *kinds* of thing. Some demand a *state* (a system must have human oversight designed in). Some demand a *process* (a risk-management system must be maintained across the lifecycle). Some demand a *document* (a technical documentation file must exist). Some demand a *test* (accuracy and robustness must be measured against representative data). Some demand a *disclosure* (users must be informed they are interacting with an AI system). Some demand a *notification* (serious incidents must be reported within a defined window). The evidence contract on a control carries this distinction — a state-obligation needs an attestation; a process-obligation needs run-history; a document-obligation needs the document itself; a test-obligation needs the measurement result; a disclosure-obligation needs a UX artefact and a code-path snapshot; a notification-obligation needs a delivered report with recipient acknowledgement. Confusing the categories is the fastest way to fail an audit — an auditor asked to see a technical file will not accept "we have a policy about it."

**Axis 4 — What the addressee bears if they fail.** Some regimes carry administrative fines (EU AI Act — up to 7% of global turnover for the top tier); some carry private rights of action (Illinois BIPA has taught the industry what that means); some carry regulator-supervisory consequences (OCC, FCA, PRA for financial-services deployers); some carry procurement disqualification (US federal contracting under OMB M-25-21); some carry criminal exposure (very few; specific narrow cases). Consequence is what determines *risk appetite* (mod-106) and therefore what determines *how much compensating control* the architect is willing to accept in an exception (mod-103 chapter 04). Consequence lives on the *obligation* record, not on the control — the same control (say, human oversight of a hiring model) can carry very different consequences in NYC versus Illinois versus Colorado.

Every chapter that follows in this module will pin obligations to these four axes. When you meet a new regulation, the first architect question is: addressee, trigger, demand, consequence.

## The two shapes reconciliation takes on a per-control basis

The reconciliation architecture uses two shapes and no others. Every time a new obligation lands, the architect decides which shape it takes.

**Shape A — new crosswalk edge onto an existing control.** The obligation is a jurisdictional restatement of a practice the enterprise already has a control for. NIST AI RMF MEASURE 2.7 (adversarial robustness), EU AI Act Article 15 (accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity), Colorado SB24-205 §6-1-1701 risk-assessment sections that touch on robustness, and CPPA ADMT §7150 risk-assessment provisions all bear on the *same practice*. The library carries one control for adversarial-robustness testing and four crosswalk edges. The evidence contract does not change; the applicability filter grows to include Colorado deployers and California ADMT-scope systems. This is the *default* shape — always try this before shape B, because shape A avoids catalogue bloat and drift.

**Shape B — new control (with its own crosswalk).** The obligation demands something the library does not already carry. EU AI Act Article 73 serious-incident reporting is a good example — the *reporting* practice (specific window, specific recipient, specific content) does not overlap cleanly with any pre-existing NIST or ISO obligation the library was carrying. A new control is authored (`AIC-INC-042 Serious-incident report to competent authority within 15 days`), the crosswalk names Article 73, the applicability filter names *provider* addressee and *high-risk* system tier and *jurisdictions in EU AI Act scope*, and the evidence contract names the report artefact plus the delivered-notification acknowledgement. Shape B is the exception, not the rule. If you are creating more shape-B controls than shape-A crosswalks in a quarter, the library is fragmenting.

**The two-shape discipline in one rule.** When a new obligation arrives, decompose it into atomic requirements. For each atomic requirement, ask: *does the library already carry a control whose statement, if satisfied, would discharge this requirement?* If yes, shape A. If no, shape B. The decomposition is where the intellectual work happens; the shape choice is mechanical.

## Three consequential design choices the architect makes once

The reconciliation architecture makes three choices at library-preface time (mod-102 chapter 01) that determine how well it scales. If you get these wrong on release 1, every downstream artefact inherits the mistake.

**Choice 1 — The jurisdiction taxonomy.** The applicability filter needs a controlled vocabulary of jurisdictions. "US" is not a jurisdiction — US-federal, Colorado, California, NYC, Illinois, Utah, Texas, Virginia, Connecticut, Washington are jurisdictions, and they attach different obligations. "EU" is not a jurisdiction for AI Act purposes — the EU AI Act is a Regulation and therefore binds member states uniformly, but member-state supervisory authorities enforce it, and some member-state-specific requirements (data-protection intersections, works-council obligations) attach on top. A workable taxonomy has three levels: *regime* (EU AI Act, CCPA/CPRA, Colorado SB24-205, NYC LL 144, ...), *territorial scope* (EU, member state, US state, city), and *sector overlay* (financial services, health, education, employment) which chapter `05-the-us-state-and-municipal-patchwork.md` and mod-113 build on. The filter references the regime; the regime carries its own territorial and sector metadata.

**Choice 2 — The obligation object.** Whether the library maintains a distinct *obligation record* — one record per atomic regulatory requirement — or whether obligations exist only as crosswalk edges attached to controls. The obligation-record approach costs more up front (a new schema, a new maintenance workflow) and pays back at every regulatory change: when EU AI Act Article 15 gets an implementing act, the architect updates one obligation record and lets the controls that reference it re-render. The crosswalk-only approach is cheaper on day one and painful thereafter — the same regulatory change forces a per-control edit sweep, misses controls that should have been touched, and leaves stale references behind. Chapter `07-designing-the-reconciliation-architecture.md` argues for the obligation-record approach. If the enterprise is single-jurisdiction on release 1, the crosswalk-only approach is defensible; every enterprise reading this module is not.

**Choice 3 — The deprecation path.** Regulations *change*. Articles are amended, memos are rescinded, standards are superseded, cases are decided. The library must be able to say: "as of *effective-date*, obligation *X* is *superseded-by* obligation *Y*; controls referencing *X* must migrate their crosswalk to *Y* by *migration-deadline*; the previous evidence contract remains valid for pre-*effective-date* audits." This is the deprecation-window pattern from mod-103 chapter 05, applied to obligations rather than to enterprise policy clauses. Chapter `07-designing-the-reconciliation-architecture.md` details the transition — the point here is that the *deprecation path is a first-class field on the obligation object* from day one, not a bolt-on added the first time a regulator amends something.

## The two failure modes to design against

**Failure mode 1 — the compliance ledger.** The library grows a control per regulation. `AIC-EUAI-Art9`, `AIC-EUAI-Art10`, ..., `AIC-COLO-SB205-6-1-1702`, ..., `AIC-NYC-LL144-1`, ... Every quarter another regime lands and 30 new controls appear. Nothing tests them cleanly because the same underlying practice is measured in three places, half the controls are inactive on any given system, and the applicability filter reads like a treaty. The two-shape rule (`A` by default, `B` by exception) is what prevents this. When someone proposes shape B, the architect asks *what practice does this actually add?* If the answer is "reporting to a specific regulator on a specific timeline," shape B is warranted. If the answer is "the same robustness testing, said in a European accent," shape A is warranted.

**Failure mode 2 — the deferral to legal.** The library carries no reconciliation architecture at all and defers to legal for every question. Every system launch waits for a legal opinion memo; every regulatory change spawns a memo update; the architect's role degenerates to "person who converts memos to compliance tickets." The evidence contract fragments across legal opinions, the audit trail becomes a mail-thread archive, and internal audit cannot test conformance because there is nothing testable. The fix is what mod-102 already argues: legal owns interpretation; the architect owns the testable-control expression of the interpretation; the two collaborate at fixed interfaces (chapter `07-designing-the-reconciliation-architecture.md` names them).

## Summary

Reconciliation is not "pick the strictest," not "one control per regulation," not "harmonise the regimes," and not "let legal own it." It is the architectural discipline of decomposing every obligation into atomic requirements, deciding whether each requirement is a new crosswalk edge on an existing control (shape A) or a genuinely new control (shape B), and pinning the answer to the four axes every obligation moves along: addressee, trigger, demand, consequence. The applicability filter carries the *when*; the evidence contract carries the *what*; the obligation record carries the *from-where* and the *if-it-changes*. Get these choices right at library-preface time and the enterprise can add its tenth or thirtieth regime without doubling headcount. Get them wrong and the library becomes a compliance ledger, the architect becomes a memo-converter, and every new regime is a crisis.
